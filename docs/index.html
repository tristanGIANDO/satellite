Part I · Results · Sentinel-2

Tired of bad weather?
Learn to delete it.

Two thirds of the planet is under cloud at any moment, so most of what an optical satellite sends back is weather rather than ground. Every image below is built the same way: a U-Net decides pixel by pixel whether it is looking at cloud, and a compositor keeps, for each pixel, the single cleanest observation of the month. Nothing is generated, inferred or painted in — every pixel was photographed.

The first three tiles below are the three places I have lived. That is not only sentiment: I know what each of them is supposed to look like, which is the only way I can tell a good result from a plausible one. Between them they break the pipeline in three different directions — detail, snow, colour. Then the fourth section runs the same pipeline over all 93 tiles of France at once, which breaks it in ways no single tile can.

01

Paris — where the detail has to survive

I live here, which makes this the tile I cannot lie to myself about. I know what these roofs, parks and bends of the river look like at 10 m, so anything the compositor smears, softens or invents is obvious to me before any metric says so.

The real test is underneath. A composite is stitched from several dates, so the risk is not that it still looks cloudy — it is that it goes soft, or that the joins between dates start showing as edges through the city.

And because this tile has twelve months behind it, the grading can be checked against itself: run the year, and only the season should move.

02

Isère — fields, and then mountains

I lived here, and I chose the tile for what sits in it: the Rhône corridor's fields, which are the easy half, and the Alps immediately behind them, which are the case I knew the model would not get for free. In visible light, fresh snow and a cumulus tower are the same object — bright, neutral, and lying on top of everything else.

Two arguments changed to get here — the tile code and the date range. No threshold was touched, no model retrained, no reference image chosen for the region. What changes is the weather it is handed, and that is legible in the masks: one pixel, one probability, white for cloud.

White is cloud probability near 1. The faint 256 px grid on 22 March is the tiling the model runs in, showing through where the whole scene is cloud and there is no contrast to anchor it.

And the half I came for: 17 March, the clearest day of the month at 90.0% usable, with no weather over the mountains at all — so whatever the mask finds up there is not cloud.

03

Montpellier — nothing to remove, everything to calibrate

I lived here too, and the sun is the point: the model finds almost no cloud to remove, so what is left on screen is the colour. Open sea, the Étang de Thau, limestone and old tile roofs, irrigated fields and the Cévennes behind — nearly the whole range the grading has to hold, in one frame.

04

France — the whole country, in one frame

Three tiles prove the compositor works. Ninety-three ask a different question: does any of it survive being scaled up? A country is not a big tile. It spans three UTM zones, so the tiles do not even share a projection; it holds sea, snow, cereal plains and Mediterranean rock in one histogram, so nothing calibrated on a single scene generalises; and at some point every shortcut that was invisible at 110 km becomes a visible square.

Scaling up broke three things, and each one was worth more than the fix. Dates were being chosen by their position in the month with no regard for whether the pass held any ground, so tiles near an orbit edge came out 13% filled from three cloud-free dates — nothing wrong with the weather or the model, simply no landscape in the scenes picked. Exposure and white point were being set per tile, on 93 different histograms, which is how a country ends up looking like 93 photographs. And a pixel was counted as data if any band covered it, so the fringe along each swath edge poisoned the black point: on one partial scene the blue black point came out at 0.003 instead of 0.166, and the whole swath rendered electric blue.

What survived all three fixes was a chequerboard. Every tile is composited from its own dates, each date's haze measured and subtracted from that date's own dark pixels, and what remains differs across a tile as well as between tiles. One gain and one offset per tile — which is all the colour matching fits — describes the average and leaves the rest on screen. So the correction was allowed to vary: a smooth field per tile, all 93 solved together, minimising disagreement where tiles share ground plus a Laplacian smoothness term. That is the prior Poisson blending imposes, applied to the correction rather than the image, which is what makes it affordable — 109 211 unknowns on a 3 km grid rather than 600 million pixels, solved in 17 seconds.

Nothing here was downloaded at full resolution. A 50 m output keeps one Sentinel-2 pixel in twenty-five, so the scenes were read from the reduced-resolution copies stored inside each JPEG 2000 file, over HTTP range requests — 20 GB instead of 125, and hours instead of a day.

05

How it works, in four lines

  1. Rank before downloading. Every candidate date is scored while still on the server, and only the four cleanest of a month are pulled.
  2. Segment. A four-channel U-Net — red, green, blue, near-infrared — gives every pixel a cloud probability.
  3. Veto. The model's call is backed by the cirrus band, a minimum blob size, a dilation for soft edges, and a shadow test.
  4. Composite. Among the clean looks at a pixel, the least atmospherically contaminated one wins; each new date is colour-matched to the mosaic already in place.

Figures on this page are downscaled. Full-resolution outputs are 10 980 px square and 200–280 MB each.
