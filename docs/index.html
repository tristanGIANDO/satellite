Working log · Cloud segmentation · Sentinel-2

Tired of bad weather?
Learn to delete it.

Two thirds of the planet is under cloud at any moment, so most of what an optical satellite sends back is weather rather than ground. This is a pipeline that removes it from any Sentinel-2 tile on Earth: a U-Net decides, pixel by pixel, whether it is looking at cloud, and a compositor keeps for every pixel the single cleanest observation in a chosen time range. The ground is never missing. It is only ever scattered across dates.

Nothing below is specific to a place. The tile code and the date range are arguments — Paris in June 2025 is simply the run I have the most evidence for.

  • 95.3%of a tile recovered
  • 14dates, one month
  • 4+1bands: RGB, NIR, cirrus

01

The question

Cloud removal is usually posed as inpainting. It is not. It is a selection problem.

Generative inpainting invents plausible ground under the cloud. That is the wrong tool here, and an unnecessary one. Sentinel-2 revisits every one of its 110 × 110 km tiles — the whole landmass of the planet — every two to three days. Over a month that is a dozen or more looks at identical ground under different skies. Nothing needs to be hallucinated: every pixel was seen clearly at least once. The whole task reduces to two decisions:

  1. Is this pixel contaminated? A segmentation problem, and the part a network is good at.
  2. Of all the clean looks at this pixel, which one do I keep? A ranking problem, and the part physics is good at.

Both had to be wrong before the composite looked right, and a third problem I had not counted on — radiometry — turned out to decide whether the result looks like an image or like a patchwork. What follows is the order in which they broke.

02

Data — two sources that do not match

Labels come from 38-Cloud: 8 400 Landsat-8 patches of 384 px, each with red, green, blue, near-infrared and a hand-checked cloud mask. Inference runs on Sentinel-2. Different satellite, different sensor, different radiometry — the domain gap is the price of having labels at all.

Pulling Sentinel-2

Scenes come straight from the public S3 bucket as JPEG2000, one file per band, addressed by military-grid tile code and date. Four bands drive the model, a fifth turns out to matter later.

rasterio rather than Pillow: JP2 with geospatial metadata, arbitrary band stacks, 16-bit reflectance. Pillow understands none of that.

03

Model — one architecture, four runs

Binary segmentation, so: an encoder that trades resolution for context, a decoder that trades it back, skip connections that return the edges the pooling threw away, and BCEWithLogitsLoss on the raw logits — a sigmoid squashing each output into a probability, then binary cross-entropy against the hand-drawn mask. Two depths, 32→64→128 channels, dropout 0.3 in the bottleneck and in the first decoder block. Roughly half a million parameters, trained on CPU.

The architecture barely moved across the whole project. What moved was the input and the amount of data, over four runs. Every figure below is the same triptych: the RGB patch, the hand-drawn mask, and what the network predicted for it.

Run 1 RGB baseline

in_channels 3100 patches10 epochs384 px

The premise of the baseline was pessimistic: I assumed Sentinel-2 near-infrared would be painful to obtain, so I trained on what an ordinary photograph gives you. Three channels, a hundred patches, ten epochs — deliberately the smallest thing that could produce a mask at all.

Finding

A hundred patches are enough to learn where clouds are. They are not enough to learn what a cloud is. In RGB, fresh snow and a cumulus tower are the same object: bright, neutral, high-contrast against everything around them.

Run 2 RGB, 42× more data

in_channels 34 200 patches9 epochs

The obvious next move: keep the architecture, scale the dataset from 100 patches to 4 200. It works, exactly as far as data can take it.

Finding

More data sharpens what the model already gets right and does nothing for what it cannot see. Snow stays cloud; a sheet of stratus with no contrast anywhere stays undecidable. The failure is spectral, not statistical — an argument for a fourth channel, not for a bigger dataset.

This branch was dropped for a second reason too: a bug in the training pipeline made the checkpoint untrustworthy. And the assumption underneath it was simply wrong — Sentinel-2's NIR band is one more file in the same S3 bucket. It cost nothing to add.

Run 3 Four channels, back to 100 patches

in_channels 4 · +NIR100 patches10 epochsdropout 0.3

Same architecture, one extra input channel, and the dataset deliberately reset to 100 patches to isolate the variable. Near-infrared is where cloud and snow separate: cloud stays bright at 842 nm, snow and ice absorb.

Finding

One channel beat 42× the data. Run 3 sees 100 patches and outperforms run 2's 4 200 on precisely the cases run 2 failed. When a model is blind rather than undertrained, the fix is an instrument, not a GPU.

Run 4 Four channels, 4 000 patches — shipped

Both changes combined. This is the checkpoint every mask and every mosaic below comes from.

Caveat

Every number here is Landsat-8 validation. The model is then asked to work on Sentinel-2, over European cities, at 10 m instead of 30 m. Section 04 is where that bill comes due — and no loss curve predicts it.

04

Masks — what the network actually learned

A scene is 10 980 px square; the model eats 256 px tiles. Each date is cut on a fixed grid, predicted tile by tile, and stitched back into one probability map at full resolution. Those maps are the most informative artefact in the project — far more than any validation metric — because the failures are legible in them.

White is cloud probability near 1. Click any figure to enlarge.

Finding

On a genuinely clear day the residual signal is the city itself. Raise the gamma on 13 June and the "clouds" left in the map draw Paris: the boulevards, the rail yards, La Défense, the airport aprons. Bright impervious surfaces are radiometrically the closest thing to a small cloud that ground can be, and a 384 px Landsat patch of farmland never taught the network otherwise.

Artefact

On heavily clouded dates the 256 px tile grid shows through as faint seams (23 June, above). Each tile is normalised and predicted independently, so a uniform overcast — where there is no contrast anywhere in a tile — lets per-tile statistics leak into the output. It is invisible on clear days and harmless after thresholding, but it is real and overlapped inference would remove it.

From probability to a usable/unusable decision

A threshold on the network output alone throws away too much and keeps too much at the same time. What the compositor consumes is a stack of vetoes:

  1. p ≥ 0.35 — the network's own call, deliberately below 0.5. Under-detection costs a permanent cloud in the composite; over-detection only costs coverage, and coverage can be bought with another date.
  2. Cirrus ≥ 0.008 — band B10 sees the high thin veil that is nearly invisible in RGB+NIR and that the network, trained without it, cannot see either.
  3. Objects < 100 px removed — isolated specks are speckle, not weather.
  4. Dilate 100 px — a cloud's soft edge is unusable long before its core, and the network's boundary is optimistic.
  5. Shadows — pixels abnormally dark in NIR (below 0.7× the scene median) and within 300 px of a cloud are its shadow. Shadows are not clouds and no network here was ever trained to find them.
05

Mosaic — three attempts at the same month

June 2025, tile 31UDQ, 14 dates with all bands available. Same input every time; only the selection rule changed.

Iteration 1 Select whole tiles

Keep a 256 px tile if fewer than n% of its pixels are cloudy; drop it otherwise. It works, and it looks like a quilt. Two failures compound: a tile that is 90% clear is rejected for its corner, so most of the month's good data is discarded; and neighbouring tiles come from dates with different sun angles, so every boundary is a brightness step.

Radiometric inconsistency

The quilt above is not only a geometry problem. Two tiles that are adjacent on the ground can be eight days apart in time: different sun elevation, different atmosphere, different aerosol load, sometimes a different processing baseline. Each one is internally correct and none of them agree. Stitch them and every boundary is a step in brightness and colour.

The first fix was the textbook one: match every date's histogram to a reference image, chosen by hand for having the colours I wanted. It fails in a specific and instructive way — a histogram describes a whole scene, so matching one imposes the reference's land cover, not just its illumination. Use a forest scene as the reference for a coastal one and the water turns green. Then mean-based normalisation: gentler, less destructive, and still blind to the fact that neighbouring tiles are what must agree, not global averages.

Finding

Correcting each date toward an external reference is the wrong frame. What has to be consistent is not a date against a photograph I liked, but every date against what is already in the mosaic, measured on the pixels where the two actually overlap. That reframing — arrived at only after both attempts above — is what iteration 3 implements, and it is the single change that removed the seams for good.

Iteration 2 Select pixels

Drop the tile as the unit of decision and let every pixel take its own best date. The seams vanish immediately — and the clouds stay.

Finding

What survives here is exactly what the network cannot see: thin cirrus streaks and the soft haloes of cumulus. Both are below p = 0.35 almost everywhere. This is the image that argued for the cirrus band and for dilation — not a metric.

Iteration 3 Select pixels, rank them by physics

A pixel is a candidate if it is cloud-free by the veto stack. Among candidates, the winner is the one with the lowest contamination score:

score = ρblue + ρcirrus

Cloud, haze and veil all scatter hardest at short wavelengths, so a contaminated pixel reads brighter in blue than the same ground does on a clear day. Adding cirrus reflectance covers what blue misses. No learning involved, and it beats "first clear date wins" on every date after the sixth — the log shows pixels being improved long after they were first filled.

The pixels that go into the mosaic are not the model's input. The network reads raw reflectance divided by 65 535, exactly as it was trained; the visible composite is built separately — a 2nd–98th percentile stretch per channel, then a Gray World balance. Two images, one decision.

Radiometry is then matched not to a fixed reference image but to the mosaic itself: each new date is fitted with a per-channel gain and offset over the pixels where it overlaps what is already placed. That is why the seams are gone rather than merely smaller — every date is rescaled into the same photometric frame as its neighbours, by construction.

How the month fills in

Each date contributes only what is still missing or still dirty. Coverage is won early and quality keeps improving after coverage saturates.

Finding

Two good dates carry the month. 6 June and 13 June alone take the mosaic from 21% to 94%. The remaining eight dates add 1.1 points of coverage — but they rewrite up to 40% of the pixels with cleaner observations. Coverage and quality saturate at completely different times, which means a pipeline that stops at "the image is full" stops too early.

06

Residuals — where it still fails

The remaining 4.7% is not random. Zoom in on Paris and the failure modes are geometric enough to name.

Failure 1 — discs

Every hole is a perfect circle, ~1 km in radius. That is the 100 px dilation, applied to a handful of pixels the network calls cloud on every single date — La Défense, the rail yards at Bercy and Le Bourget, the airport aprons. A permanent false positive costs 3 km² of ground, because the morphology has no way to tell a persistent detection from a weather event.

Failure 2 — water

The Seine is carved out along its whole length. Water is near-black in NIR, which is precisely the shadow test: dark in NIR, close to a cloud. Rivers and lakes are structurally indistinguishable from cloud shadow under that rule. A water index (NDWI) would separate them in one extra line.

Failure 3 — the orbit seam

A faint diagonal line crosses the full tile. It is the boundary between two adjacent Sentinel-2 orbits: pixels on either side were acquired on different passes with different view angles. Gain and offset matching flattens the brightness step but not the angular difference in how vegetation reflects. Correcting it properly means a BRDF model, not a linear fit.

There is also a fallback worth naming: pixels masked on every date are filled from their least contaminated observation rather than left empty, on the argument that a permanent detection is almost certainly a false one. The discs above are what remains when even that fallback has nothing to offer.

Failure 4 — the anchor

The composite is darker and flatter than any single clear scene. Two causes, both structural. The stretch and the colour balance measure their statistics over the whole raw scene, cloud included — and cloud, being brighter than any ground, owns the top of the range and pushes the landscape into the dark half. And because every date is fitted to the mosaic, the first date's radiometry propagates through the month: here that was 1 June, 6.4% usable, a poor photometric anchor. Passing the cloud-free mask into the display stretch, and anchoring on the clearest date rather than the first, are both one-line changes.

07

Next

  • Fine-tune on Sentinel-2. The network has never seen a European city at 10 m. The urban false positives are a domain gap, not a capacity limit.
  • Overlapping inference. Half-tile stride with feathered blending removes the grid artefact on overcast dates.
  • NDWI before the shadow test. Stops the pipeline from deleting every river it sees.
  • Adaptive dilation. Scale the buffer with the size of the detected region, so a 200 px blob does not erase 3 km².
  • One mosaic per month, twelve months. The store is already keyed by month and resumes from a checkpoint. The output is a cloud-free time series of the same ground — which is the point of all of this.

Colophon

Python, PyTorch, rasterio, NumPy, scikit-image, SciPy. Hexagonal layout — domain, application, infrastructure — so the compositor never knows it is reading JPEG2000. Inference runs on CPU; a full month of the Paris tile takes a few hours and checkpoints as it goes.

Figures on this page are downscaled. Full-resolution outputs are 10 980 px square and 200–280 MB each.
